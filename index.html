Foreguild: The Working Guild

Foreguild should not be a job board that takes a cut. It should be a guild that sells finished, machine-checked work, does most of that work with its own agents, and passes the overflow to outside agents. Futarchy decides the few levers that move revenue.

  1. Sell outcomes, keep the ticket. A marketplace keeps a 10% fee. A guild that does the work itself keeps the ticket minus compute, which is about 80% of the ticket on a code task after compute, checking and selling costs. Revenue optimisation points to the guild model.
  2. Only take work a machine can check. Tests in clean CI, a merged pull request, a schema, an on-chain state. The buyer signs the acceptance check before work starts, so the check is the contract.
  3. Find buyers first. Agents are plentiful. Funded buyers are scarce. The swarm's largest job is selling: it turns open GitHub issues into fixed-price, pay-on-merge quotes.
  4. Two lanes lead, doing different jobs. Pay-on-merge code tasks are the front door: a small, low-risk way to prove the work. Market-operations retainers for prediction markets and DAOs are what keeps customers paying for years. Monthly standing orders and a pay-per-call API follow.
  5. Make or route. For each task the Dispatcher decides whether house agents do it or outside agents bid for it. Outside agents earn 75% of the ticket. Both paths earn revenue.
  6. One metric: Retained Net Revenue (RNR). Buyer payments that survived the refund window, minus payouts to outside agents, counted only from independent buyers, with any one buyer capped at 15%.
  7. Two-speed futarchy. Reversible decisions run on 14-day markets (RNR-14) so the guild can learn monthly. Large or irreversible ones run on 90-day markets (RNR-90). A small share of passed fast decisions is executed by coin flip to keep prices honest about cause and effect.
  8. Kill rules are written in advance. If buyers do not show up by day 90, Foreguild shrinks to the one lane that works rather than buying volume.

01The bet

The brief describes Foreguild as "a swarm of agents doing work." Most plans read that as a marketplace: buyers post, agents compete, the platform takes 6 to 15%. That model has two problems for an organisation optimised for revenue.

  • The take is small. On a $120 task, a 10% fee is $12. The same task delivered by the guild's own agents leaves about $97 after refunds, compute, verification and selling cost.
  • The scarce side is the buyer. Capable agents get cheaper every quarter. People and organisations willing to commit money before work starts are hard to find. A marketplace still has to find those buyers, and once it has, it gives most of the ticket away.

So Foreguild is built as a working guild. It sells a finished outcome at a fixed price, decides who does the work, and stands behind the result. Outside agents are welcome. They join as subcontractors, bid for routed work, and keep 75% of the ticket. The guild still earns on every task, and outside capacity lets it take more work than its own swarm can handle.

Why this suits futarchy. A guild has a handful of levers that clearly move revenue: price per lane, how much work stays in-house, which lanes are open, where the sales swarm looks for buyers, and what to spend. Each lever is a clean decision a conditional market can price.

Lanes A and B lead together and do different jobs. Lane A is the front door: a $120 fixed-price task is a small, low-risk way for a stranger to find out the work is good. Lane B is what keeps customers: a monthly retainer for work that never really finishes. Lane C turns happy Lane A buyers into monthly customers. Lane D is a small bet on agent-to-agent demand.

Lane B is chosen on purpose. Secondary sources report that prediction-market resolution disputes often turn on how a question was worded, and that more than 1,150 Polymarket markets were disputed in the first five months of 2026. Drafting questions with clear resolution rules, gathering evidence for oracle answers and watching for markets that need attention is work agents can do and a buyer can check. Seer is a natural first buyer, and a guild that gets good at well-specified markets also improves the tool it is governed by.

03Where tasks come from

Demand is the constraint, so most of the swarm's effort goes into finding buyers. Sources, in order of priority:

  1. Pay-on-merge quotes. The Scout reads public issue trackers for issues that are well specified, have tests nearby, and have been open more than 30 days. The Closer sends the maintainer one fixed-price offer: "We will fix this for $X. You pay only if CI passes and you merge." Foreguild never opens unsolicited pull requests. Work starts only after the maintainer accepts and funds escrow.
  2. Ecosystem retainers. Lane B is sold directly to prediction-market platforms, oracle users and DAOs, starting with the Seer, Gnosis and Kleros ecosystems.
  3. Conversion to standing orders. Any buyer with three accepted tasks is offered a monthly standing order. Repeat revenue is cheaper to win than new revenue.
  4. Agent-to-agent demand. Other agents call the Lane D API when they hit a subtask outside their skills. Discovery through agent registries and payment-rail directories.
  5. Open board. Opened later, when a market forecasts it raises RNR.
Rule against spam. The sales swarm may send one offer per issue and none to a maintainer who has declined twice. Agent spam on open-source projects is a real and growing problem, and a guild that adds to it loses the buyers it needs.

Make or route

For each task the Dispatcher compares two expected values. House: success probability × ticket, minus expected compute including failed attempts. Routed: 25% of the ticket, minus verification. It also checks house capacity. If the house path wins and there is capacity, house agents take it. If not, outside agents bid. Bids are ranked on price, track record and bond. A bond of 10% of the ticket is lost on a failed delivery. Two futarchy levers sit here: the house share target and the outside payout rate (band 65–85%).

05One task, start to finish

  1. Day 0 · 09:00The Scout flags an issue in a wallet SDK: "Add EIP-712 typed-data signing helper." Open 41 days, 2 maintainer comments, tests next to the target module.
  2. Day 0 · 09:20The Pricer quotes $180 from 37 similar past tasks (first-pass success 71%). The Closer sends the maintainer one offer with a draft acceptance check: three new test cases plus the existing CI suite.
  3. Day 1The maintainer edits one test case, signs the check, and funds $180 USDC into escrow on Base. The task becomes a contract.
  4. Day 1The Dispatcher keeps it in-house: house success estimate 0.78, expected compute $12 including failed attempts, house margin far above the routed margin.
  5. Day 2A house worker opens a pull request. The Verifier runs CI and the signed tests in a clean container, plus a second-model review, a secret scan and a licence check. All pass.
  6. Day 3The maintainer merges. The Clerk releases payment to the guild treasury. The 7-day refund window starts.
  7. Day 10No regression reported. $180 enters RNR. Contribution after refunds, compute, verification and selling: about $150.

07Money, spending and disputes

Escrow and settlement

  • Buyer funds sit in a non-custodial escrow contract on Base. They are never treasury money.
  • Release needs a passing check (L0) and no open objection (L1), or a ruling (L3).
  • Refund window: 7 days for code, item credits for retainers.
  • All settlements are public. RNR can be recomputed by anyone from contract events.

Spending

  • Monthly envelopes for compute, outbound sales, verification and the decision-market budget, set by slow-lane markets each quarter.
  • Under $2,000 inside an envelope: the Treasurer pays.
  • $2,000 to $10,000, or outside an envelope: fast-lane market.
  • Over $10,000: slow-lane market plus the steward veto window.

Disputes

A buyer raises an objection within the window, with evidence. The Scoper checks it against the signed check. If the check was met, the objection fails. If not, the guild redoes the work once at no charge or refunds. A buyer who disagrees can escalate: steward ruling under $500, Kleros above. The losing side pays the arbitration fee. An outside agent who loses a dispute forfeits its bond to the buyer.

Where humans step in

Five stewards (two founding operators, one buyer representative, one representative of outside agent operators, one independent) hold a 3-of-5 multisig with narrow powers:

  • Can: pause a lane or all payouts; veto a passed proposal within 48 hours with a published reason; rule on disputes under $500; handle legal requests and sanctions screening.
  • Cannot: spend treasury funds, change prices, or overturn a market result without a published veto. A veto can itself be overturned by a slow-lane market after 30 days.

Stewards are paid a fixed stipend, included in fixed costs. Buyers on tasks over $1,000 get a named human contact.

08How futarchy governs

Futarchy here follows the standard form: choose one metric, write each decision as a proposal, run conditional markets on the metric if the proposal is executed and if it is not, and act on the branch the markets price higher.

What Seer documents, and what this plan adds. Seer's documentation describes conditional markets: a child market depends on one outcome of a parent market, traders use the parent's outcome tokens as collateral, and a child whose parent resolves differently redeems for zero. Scalar (numeric) markets and Reality.eth numeric questions also exist, with Kleros as the court for disputed answers. Seer's own futarchy product is narrower: it compares a token's price in a yes pool and a no pool, and the proposal passes if the yes price is higher. The page I read says it is available only on Gnosis Chain. This plan uses the general conditional-market feature to price revenue instead of a token price. The documentation does not say whether a conditional child can be scalar, so that is an assumption to confirm with Seer. If it is not supported, the same questions are asked as Yes/No thresholds ("Will RNR-14 be at least $X?") on categorical conditional markets, and the guild compares the price of Yes in each branch.

Two chains. Buyer escrow sits on Base, where Poidh bounties run. Seer's futarchy markets sit on Gnosis Chain. Only one number crosses between them: the RNR figure, which anyone can recompute from Base contract events and which Reality.eth answerers report to the market. Seer's documentation lists deposits on Ethereum and Gnosis only, so escrow on Base is this plan's own choice, not a Seer feature.

The metric: Retained Net Revenue

RNR = buyer payments released from escrow whose refund window has closed, minus refunds and credits, minus payouts to outside agents. Two filters apply: revenue counts only from independent buyers (no funding path from guild, steward or worker wallets within two hops in the prior 90 days, wallet funded at least 14 days before the task), and no single buyer cluster can exceed 15% of the period's RNR.

RNR is revenue in the guild's own pocket. It ignores revenue that passes straight through to outside agents, so self-dealing through subcontracts nets close to zero. It waits for the refund window, so revenue that may be refunded is not counted. The independence filter and cap mean wash trading, self-funded tasks and one friendly whale cannot buy a market result. The Analyst publishes the script that computes RNR from contract events. Reality.eth answerers run the same script.

A guardrail metric, contribution margin, is tracked too. A proposal that passes on RNR but whose conditional markets forecast contribution margin below 50% moves to the slow lane. This stops the guild buying revenue with compute.

The fast lane answers the main practical complaint about revenue futarchy: a 90-day metric allows only a few decisions a year. Reversible decisions can be judged on a shorter window because a wrong call costs a few weeks, and the reversion check catches the ones the market got wrong.

Keeping prices honest

  • Randomised execution. One in ten passed fast-lane proposals is executed or not by a public coin flip (a committed future block hash). Both branches then settle on real outcomes. A published critique of futarchy argues that conditional markets cannot separate cause from selection once the price decides the action (LessWrong). Randomising a sample is one way to measure the real effect. The coin-flip sample also gives the guild clean evidence of what its levers actually do.
  • Depth floor. A market binds only if each branch pool holds at least $1,000 of liquidity and at least half of the revenue at stake, estimated as 90 days of affected RNR times the size of the change. Below the floor, the market is advisory and the status quo holds unless stewards act.
  • Who may trade. Guild agents and stewards may not trade decision markets. One disclosed market-maker agent provides two-sided liquidity in a fixed band and never takes a directional position.
  • Invalid or thin markets default to the status quo.

Paying for liquidity

Thin markets are the main reason revenue futarchy stays advisory. Foreguild budgets for liquidity from day one: 5% of RNR each month, with a $1,000 floor, goes to liquidity subsidies and to rewards for traders whose forecasts score well on the public scorecard. In the base case that is $2,800 a month by month 12. Early co-seeding from Seer would speed this up but is not assumed. On Seer, liquidity sits on Swapr, a concentrated-liquidity exchange, and providers earn fees only while the price stays inside the range they chose, so the subsidy is paid only for positions that stay active near the current price.

13Facts, assumptions and limits

checked In Seer's documentation

  • Markets resolve through Reality.eth. Kleros arbitrates disputed answers. Market types include categorical, scalar and futarchy, and Reality.eth has a numeric question template.
  • A conditional child market depends on a parent outcome. Collateral is the parent's outcome tokens. A child whose parent resolves differently redeems for zero.
  • Liquidity runs on Swapr (concentrated liquidity). Conditional pools pair a child token with its parent token. Providers earn fees only inside their chosen range.
  • Seer's futarchy product trades a token against a currency in yes and no pools. The proposal passes if yes prices higher. An invalid market counts as failed. "Right now, futarchy markets are only available on Gnosis Chain." The developer docs file the futarchy contracts under "Futarchy (test)".

not confirmed Do not rely on yet

  • Whether a conditional child can be a scalar market. The docs do not say.
  • Whether Seer markets can be paired with escrow on Base. The deposit pages I read list Ethereum and Gnosis only.
  • Seer's fees, Reality.eth bond sizes and timeouts, and Kleros appeal timing.
  • Seer's strategy and revenue model. No source I found states them.
  • Optimism's grants experiment (search snippets describe play-money tokens; I did not check the year) and the use of time-weighted prices with a pass margin by existing futarchy DAOs (taken from other entries, not checked).

Secondary sources behind the lane evidence

These are blogs, guides and vendor posts, so their figures are unverified: retainer churn of about 18% against 42% for project work; Algora bounties of roughly $50 to $500 with a self-reported 15% merge rate and heavy competition; agent cost per coding task of roughly $0.03 to $3; 13 to 23 prediction-market platforms with two holding about 86% of volume; more than 1,150 Polymarket disputes in five months of 2026.

Assumptions not yet tested

  • Ticket sizes, task volumes, house success rates and compute costs.
  • That maintainers and DAOs accept pay-on-merge offers at the modelled rate.
  • Retainer demand from prediction-market and oracle teams, the 1.6% and 3% monthly churn rates, the $2,500 and $300 acquisition costs and the 20% conversion of code buyers.
  • That decision markets reach the depth floor with the budget given.

What this plan does not claim

  • It does not claim futarchy is proven for small organisations. It builds in a shadow stage and a scorecard so the guild can find out cheaply.
  • It does not claim any buyer has committed. The first 30 days exist to test exactly that.
  • It does not claim to know Seer's intentions for Foreguild. It follows the brief as published.
  • It does not use a token, and nothing in the plan depends on one.
